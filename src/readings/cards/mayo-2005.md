    pages: "300–302"
    thesis: "Like Neyman and Pearson, Peirce gives probabilities to methods, not to conclusions: how often a form of inference would lead us right. Priors over hypotheses are not statistical facts, and subjective ones only measure how a new idea fits our prejudices. Where Peirce goes beyond today's error statistician is in asking what a test teaches in the case at hand, not only how it behaves in the long run."

  - n: "3"
    title: "So why is justifying Peirce's SCT thought to be so problematic?"
    pages: "302–304"
    thesis: "The critics read quantitative induction as the straight rule and qualitative induction as hypothetico-deduction, and ask a self-correcting method to converge on the truth and to supply better hypotheses mechanically. Both readings miss what Peirce meant: induction is reliable testing, and its worth is how well it uncovers mistakes."
  - n: "4"
    title: "Peircean induction as severe testing"
    pages: "304–306"
    thesis: "For Peirce a test looks for consequences that would be surprising if the hypothesis were false, and a hypothesis that survives earns positive standing. That is severity: what is graded is not the support for H but the capacity of the test to catch the errors an inference to H declares absent."
    steps:
      - t: "Testing, not agreement"
        d: "A test examines the consequences that would be unlikely were the hypothesis false, not how well the facts accord with it."
        claims: [C040]
      - t: "Peirce against Popper"
        d: "Popper keeps only refutation and will not call a well-corroborated hypothesis warranted. Peirce wants what a passed test positively tells us."
        key: true
        quote: "Peirce, unlike Popper, is primarily interested not in falsifying claims but in the positive pieces of information provided by tests"
        page: "304"
      - t: "The severity requirement"
        d: "H passes a severe test when the data accord with H and the procedure would very probably have signalled an error had H been false. The higher the probative capacity, the more severely H has passed."
        key: true
      - t: "Two corrections to the critics"
        d: "Quantitative and qualitative induction differ only in whether severity can be measured or only judged. And the thesis has to justify accepting what passes, not only rejecting what fails."
  - n: "5"
    title: "The path from qualitative to quantitative induction"
    pages: "306–307"
    thesis: "Peirce's orders of induction are steps on one scale of severity, ranked by how much the confirmed prediction runs against what we would have expected without the hypothesis."
    steps:
      - t: "First order: crude induction"
        d: "No evidence against H, so provisionally adopt H. It corrects itself only with a bang, and Peirce keeps it out of science: absence of counter-evidence is evidence only if the counter-evidence would have shown up."
      - t: "Second order: qualitative induction"
        d: "Fulfilled predictions, weaker when they only repeat what suggested the hypothesis, stronger when they are novel. Strength is judged, not measured."
      - t: "Third order: statistical induction"
        d: "When the surprise can be quantified, the test has an error probability and the induction becomes quantitative."
  - n: "6"
    title: "Quantitative and qualitative induction: significance test reasoning"
    pages: "308–311"
    thesis: "A significance test is quantitative severity, and its self-correction is not climbing probabilities but checking whether an agreement recurs as often as chance would make it recur. Its assumptions are criticised by the same reasoning, informally; and data dredging wrecks severity, which is why randomisation and predesignation are rules of induction."
    steps:
      - t: "Quantitative severity"
        d: "If the null were true, a result this significant would rarely occur; when it does, the alternative passes with severity one minus the p-value. A wrong rejection shows itself because the result fails to recur."
        claims: [C041]
      - t: "Semi-formal or qualitative severity"
        d: "Observational studies of hormone replacement therapy could not separate its effect from the advantages of the women who took it, so agreement with the hypothesis of a benefit was no severe test. The strongest severity arguments need no formal assessment."
      - t: "Predesignation"
        d: "Testing the one factor out of twenty that looked impressive turns a nominal 5% into roughly 64%: the selection belongs to the test."

    title: "Random sampling and the uniformity of nature"
    pages: "314–316"
    thesis: "Severity needs a link between data and the process that produced them, but not the uniformity of nature. Randomness need only be approximate, departures from it weaken a conclusion without destroying it, and different methods of selection that err differently and still agree point to the truth."
    steps:

      - t: "Different selections"
